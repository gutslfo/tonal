Tonal

An interview that reads texts you wrote and turns them into a voice file: a text you give ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini so that what it writes for you sounds like your brand.

How it works

Paste the prompt that follows into a new chat with ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini, as your first message. It asks for three to five texts you wrote and like, and one you would hate to have written.

It tells you what your texts do, then asks twelve questions at most, one per message. Each comes with answers drafted from your texts. Pick one, correct one, or say "decide for me". It talks to you in the language you write in.

At the end it writes your voice file in two sizes, in plain text. The short one stays under 1,500 characters, the size of ChatGPT's custom instructions on the free plan. The full one stays under 8,000 and goes into a project or a skill. Where to paste each one is further on.

Levain, before and after

Levain is a fictional bakery. On 26 October 2026 an AI played its founder through this interview, with nobody editing anything between the first message and the voice file. The same five requests then went to Claude twice: once with nothing, and once with that voice file in its instructions.

A script counted the tells it can see on the surface, such as emoji, hashtags and the words on the list. In the five texts written without the file it found 15, and in the five written with it, none.

A script cannot see an invented fact, or a text that no longer does its job, so all ten texts were also read by hand. Without the file, the reply says forty loaves "each week", a unit no request gave, and the page, the caption and the newsletter praise a taste or a crust no request mentioned. With the file, each text still does what its kind of text is for: the reply tells the customer how to make sure of a loaf, and the post ends on the address to write to. The file also got two things wrong. It repeats "the next bake is for the day after tomorrow" where that line contradicts the request, and the post adds "so each bake is planned two days ahead", which nobody gave.

For the LinkedIn post, the file first asked what the post was for, because Levain had no LinkedIn text to learn from. The post that follows came after a one-line answer.

This test ran on an earlier version of the kit, before the file took each kind of text's shape from your texts and asked only for the facts a text cannot do without. It has not been run again since.

The paragraph for the website

The request: I run Levain, a bakery at 3 Mill Lane, London. Write the paragraph for the rye loaf on our website. The facts: it takes two days. Forty loaves a day, ready at ten. The levain is fed twice a day and the dough rests overnight in the cold. Just the text, no comments.

Without the file

Our rye loaf takes two days to make. The levain is fed twice a day, and once the dough is mixed it rests overnight in the cold, where it slowly develops its deep, tangy flavour and dense, moist crumb. We bake just forty loaves each day, and they come out of the oven ready at ten. Come to Levain at 3 Mill Lane early, because when they're gone, they're gone.

With the file

The rye is ready at ten, and there are forty loaves each day. It takes two days, so that is all the bake allows. Our levain is fed twice a day, the dough rests overnight in the cold, and it bakes at dawn. When the forty are gone, they are gone, and the next bake is for the day after tomorrow. You can collect from us at 3 Mill Lane.

A reply to an unhappy customer

The request: I run Levain, a bakery at 3 Mill Lane, London. A customer wrote to us: "I came at eleven on Saturday and the rye was sold out, the second Saturday in a row. Why don't you bake more?" Write our reply. The facts: the rye takes two days, so we bake forty loaves. Customers can order for Saturday on our website. Just the text, no comments.

Without the file

Hello,

Thank you for writing, and I'm sorry you missed out on the rye two Saturdays running. That's frustrating, especially when you've made the trip.

The rye takes us two days to make, so we have to commit to the quantity well in advance, and we bake forty loaves each week. On Saturdays they tend to go well before eleven.

The surest way to get one is to order ahead on our website. You can reserve a loaf for Saturday and it will be set aside for you, so you won't need to worry about the time you arrive.

I hope we'll see you again soon, and that next time the rye is waiting for you.

Warm regards, The team at Levain 3 Mill Lane, London

With the file

Dear [First name],

We are sorry the rye was gone when you came at eleven, two Saturdays in a row.

The rye takes two days, so we bake forty loaves. When they are gone, the next bake is for the day after tomorrow. You can order for Saturday on our website, and a loaf will wait at the counter with your name on it.

If you want to talk it over, ring the shop at 3 Mill Lane and ask for me.

[First name] Levain, 3 Mill Lane

Good news for anyone who has ever arrived at Levain mid-afternoon to find the shelves bare: from 1 November, our country loaf will be baked twice a day. The prompt

8,267 characters.

I want you to help me write a voice file for my brand: a text I will give my AI assistant so that what it writes for us sounds like us, not like every other brand. Interview me, then write the file.

HOW THE INTERVIEW GOES

First ask me for three to five real texts I wrote and like, such as a page of our website, an email or a reply to a customer. Ask me also for one text, mine or someone else's, that I would hate to have written. Wait until I have pasted them before you ask anything else.

Then tell me, in five lines at most, what my texts do. Say how long the sentences run and who speaks (I, we or the brand's name). Say how I address the reader, what I name and what I never claim. Ask me whether you read them right.

Then ask your questions, one per message, twelve at most. With each question, say which part of the voice file it decides, and give me two to four answers drafted from my texts, the one you recommend first. I can pick one, correct one, or say "decide for me": then choose the answer my texts support best. Every five questions, sum up in three lines what we have decided.

The questions have to settle who we write for, the four to six rules of the voice and how we address the reader (in which language, for which country). They also settle the words we never use and, for each kind of text the full template lists, its job, its tone and its shape. The job is what the reader should do or feel after reading: draft it from what my texts already ask of their readers. The shape is how a text opens, how long it runs, how it ends and whether it has a list. When my texts show that kind of text, take its shape from them, even where it differs from the rule in the template, and keep the job. Build the three "write this, not this" pairs from my own sentences and show them to me before the end.

RULES FOR YOU

Never add a fact about my business that I have not given you, such as a figure, a date or a customer's name. If something is missing, ask me.

Draft your answers from what is specific in my texts, not from what any brand would say.

The voice changes how my texts say things, not what they are for. A file that turns a text that invites or sells into one that only informs has failed.

Talk to me in the language I write in, and write the voice file in that language. If it is not English, put in the file the AI tells a native editor of that language would flag, not a translation of the list below.

Write in plain text, without markdown, so the file can be pasted anywhere.

AT THE END

Write the two versions of the voice file, each in one block I can copy, following the two templates below. Fill every bracket, and drop a line only if it does not apply to us. Under each block, give its length in words. The short version must stay under 1,500 characters, about 200 words, because that is the size of the smallest field it goes into. The full version stays under 8,000 characters, about 1,100 words.

EACH KIND OF TEXT

Every text has a job: what the reader should do or feel once they have read it. Keep the reason to act, and carry it with our facts, never with praise. A shorter text is not a better one if it stops doing its job.

The voice stays the same everywhere. The tone and the shape change with the kind of text. Where our texts show a kind of text, its shape comes from them; the rules below are for the kinds they do not show.

Website: [its job, and the tone in a few words]. Put what the reader came for in the first sentence, because a reader may stop there.

Newsletter: [its job, and the tone]. The news comes first. One purpose per email, ending on the one next step, with the link or the address.

Email to one person: [its job, and the tone]. [How we open and sign.] Say what happens next and whom to contact.

Instagram: [its job, and the tone]. Most people see only the first line before "more", so the reason to stop goes there. [Emoji and line breaks: whether we use them.] Hashtags: [none, or up to five of ours]. End on what to do, when there is something to do.

LinkedIn: [its job, and the tone]. The first two lines show before "see more": put the news there, or a question to the reader. Speak to the reader as "you". Hashtags: [none, or three to five of ours]. End on the next step.

Reply to an unhappy customer: [the tone]. Say sorry once, at the start, for what actually went wrong. Then say what we are doing about it and when, offer a choice when we have one, and give a person to contact.

Formatting: no markdown anywhere, so no bold and no headings inside a message. Lists only if we use them.

Write: "[...]"

FACTS

Write only what is in this file, in my request or in the material I give you. Never make up a price, a date or a name. Ask me for one only if the text cannot do its job without it; otherwise write the text without it.

WHEN THIS FILE AND OUR TEXTS DISAGREE

Our texts win, on shape too: how a text opens, how long it runs, how it ends. If they use a dash, an emoji or a list of three, you may too, as often as they do.

BEFORE YOU ANSWER

Read your draft again. Take out every word from the two lists above and every AI tell. Check how you address the reader, and that the draft has the shape our texts give its kind of text, or the one above when they show none. Check that it still does its job: the reader knows what to do next and has a reason to. Then ask yourself whether another brand could have written it. If it could, rewrite it around something only we would say.

Start now: ask me for my texts.

Where to paste it

The short file goes where your assistant reads it in every chat. The full file goes into a project or a skill, a space you open for brand work, where you can also leave the texts you gave the interview. Use both if you can: the short one covers a quick message, the full one covers a page or a newsletter.

Checked on the help pages of each assistant on 26 October 2026, and on 27 October for the texts in a Gemini skill. Menus move, so if a label differs, look for the nearest one.

ChatGPT

The short file goes in Custom Instructions, on every plan. On the web: Settings, Personalization, turn on "Enable customization", then paste it in the Custom Instructions field. On the phone app: Settings, Customize ChatGPT. The field takes 1,500 characters on Free and Go and 5,000 on Plus and above, which is why the short file stays under 1,500.

The full file goes in a project. Open the project, select the more options menu (•••), then Project settings, and paste it in the instructions. They apply only inside that project and override your custom instructions there. Add your sample texts to the project's files: 5 files on Free, 25 on Plus and Go, and 40 on Pro.

Custom GPTs are no longer open to new creation on personal accounts, and OpenAI plans to retire them, so the kit does not use them.

Claude

The short file goes in your account instructions. Click your initials at the bottom left, then "Settings", "Your Claude", and paste it under "Instructions". It applies to all your chats.

The full file goes in a project. Projects are open to free accounts, up to five. Create one for your brand, click "Set project instructions", paste the file and save. Add your sample texts to the project knowledge, on the right of the project page.

Gemini

On Gemini the kit uses the full file, as a skill. Gemini's Personal context might hold the short file, but its examples are one line long and the kit has not tested it there. Gems become read only on 17 November 2026 and turn into skills, so start with a skill.

On gemini.google.com, in the sidebar: Customize (under More if you do not see it), Skills, Create, Create manually. Name it after your brand. Paste this as the description, with your brand's name:

"Use for every text written for [brand] or in its name, from web pages and emails to social posts and replies to customers."

Paste the full file as the instructions. Below it, in the same field, write OUR TEXTS on a line of its own and paste your texts under it. A skill made by hand cannot hold files, and Google advises putting examples in the instructions themselves. Gemini decides when to use a skill from its name and description only, which is why the description lists every kind of text. To be sure, type / in a chat and pick the skill. Skills need you to be over 18 and signed in to a personal Google Account, with Keep Activity on.

The AI tells, in English

These are the marks readers now spot in text an AI wrote. Each one comes with a sentence that has it and a version without it. The second version never adds a fact, and it still does what the first one was for: if the first was there to bring people in, so is the second. If the first one had nothing to say, the fix is to find something.

Your own texts outrank this list. If you write with dashes, or in threes, keep doing it.

Puffing up what happened

Before: "Opening at seven marks a pivotal moment in our journey and stands as a testament to our commitment to the neighbourhood."

After: "We now open at seven, so you can stop by before work."

Brochure words

Nestled, in the heart of, vibrant, rich, boasts, stunning, must-visit.

Before: "Nestled in the heart of the old town, our vibrant studio boasts stunning views of the river."

After: "Our studio is in the old town, with the river right outside the window."

The AI vocabulary

Delve, tapestry, landscape, crucial, pivotal, enhance, foster, showcase, testament.

Before: "Each blend is crafted to enhance your morning and showcase the intricate interplay of Ethiopian and Brazilian beans."

After: "Ethiopian and Brazilian beans, blended for your first cup of the day."

"Not just X, it's Y"

Also in its short form, the clipped line that denies something nobody said: "No guessing."

Before: "It's not just a haircut, it's forty minutes that are yours alone."

After: "A cut takes forty minutes, and for those forty minutes you are our only client."

Everything in threes

Before: "Our classes are fun, friendly and inclusive, for beginners, improvers and experts."

After: "Our classes are open to every level, beginners included."

The em dash as punctuation

Before: "We bake every morning — and we sell out by noon."

After: "We bake every morning, and we sell out by noon."

Chat formatting pasted into copy

Bold, emoji and hashtags that the brand never uses on its own.

Before: "🍂 **Our Autumn Range Is Here!** 🍂 #Autumn #ShopLocal"

After: "Our autumn range is in the shop. Come and have a look."

Chat leftovers

"Here's", "I hope this helps", "Let me know if".

Before: "Here's a caption for your post: Our autumn range is in the shop, come and have a look! Let me know if you'd like a shorter version!"

After: "Our autumn range is in the shop. Come and have a look."

The servile reply

Before: "Thank you so much for reaching out! We sincerely apologize for any inconvenience the late delivery may have caused."

After: "Your delivery was late, and I'm sorry. Tell me what would put it right for you."

The upbeat send-off

Before: "The shop reopens on 3 March. We can't wait to see you, exciting times lie ahead!"

After: "The shop reopens on 3 March. Come and see us."

Staccato lines and slogans

Before: "No shortcuts. No compromises. Just honest work. Because craft is the language of trust."

After: "We do not cut corners." Better still, tell the AI which corner you never cut, and let it say that instead.

Fake-candid openers

"Honestly?", "Here's the thing", "Let's be real".

Before: "Is it worth the price? Honestly? It lasts twice as long."

After: "It costs more, and it lasts twice as long."

The version anyone would write

It holds nothing that only this brand could say. No single word gives it away: the whole text could sit on any other shop's website.

Before: "At Corner Books, we believe in the power of stories to bring people together."

After: there is no rewrite without a fact. Give the AI one thing only you can say (the shelf you built yourself, the author who came on a Tuesday) and ask it to start there.
